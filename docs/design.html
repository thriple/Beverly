Design draft · 2026-11-14

Beverly

A standalone, no-code-first editor for making 3D Bevy games for Windows. The name comes from Bevy. Last Ascent is its first game. This page fills in the open topics from your list, adds the ones that were missing, and sketches the main screen.

Logic: parts + noodles; Rust only for new kinds of parts Export: prebuilt player (instant zip) or compile (local / GitHub) Platform: Windows 3D only Assets: glTF, sounds, images + basic shapes Built with: Bevy + egui; projects are folders of text files

One timeline, everywhere

Many parts of the editor are really "things happening over time", so they all get the same timeline widget. Learn it once in the Sequence part and it works the same in animatics, light effects, weather, spawn waves and menu transitions.

Your ambush chain as a timeline. Clips are things that last (the camera focus, a dialog line), keys are values at a moment (diamonds, with the curve opened under Shake), and markers are moments that fire wires out to other parts.

Same everywhere

One anatomy, one set of controls

  • Tracks (one per thing being animated), clips (things that last; drag the edges to trim, Alt-drag to copy), keys (values at a moment, with an optional curve lane), and markers (moments that fire wires).
  • With the cursor over the timeline, the viewport keys carry over: wheel zooms toward the cursor, middle-drag pans, Space plays and pauses.
  • Scrubbing previews the level live in the viewport.
  • It docks in the bottom panel, which grows taller while it's open. The selected clip's settings show at its right.
Plugs into wiring

A timeline is a part

  • Inputs: Play, Stop, Pause, Jump to. Outputs: Started, Finished, plus one per marker. So a timeline wires into noodles like anything else.
  • A clip can be another timeline, such as an animatic inside a sequence. Double-click to dive in; a breadcrumb takes you back out. That's your "stages that nest" idea, applied to time.
  • The ruler can count metres instead of seconds, for camera rails and moving platforms: the same widget, measuring distance along the path.
Reused in

Every "over time" feature

  • Sequence part (its list view is the timeline read top to bottom)
  • Animatics (camera cuts, character recordings, subtitles)
  • Light FX (flicker, pulse, colour shifts) and weather changes
  • Particle effects (bursts, size and colour over a particle's life)
  • Spawn waves, camera rails, moving platforms
  • Dialog, music and ambience cues
  • Menu transitions and HUD warnings

Animating on the stage

The timeline stays in the bottom panel; it's too fiddly to use as an object in the world. What moves into 3D is a toggle overlay for animating: the paths things travel, widgets on each key, and curve handles to shape the path. It's off unless you're animating, so the stage stays clean.

A camera path being animated. The keys, handles and playhead in the world are the same ones on the timeline below: select a key in either place and it's selected in both.

Drawing paths

Click points, or fly it

  • With the overlay on, click points on surfaces or in the air to lay down a path; each point becomes a key.
  • Or fly the path in Builder movement while recording, and the editor smooths what you flew into a few keys.
  • Paths can close into loops, for patrols and moving platforms.
Shaping

Keys and curve handles

  • Each key has a move widget. Its two tangent handles (violet) bend the path in and out of the key; Alt-drag breaks them for a sharp corner.
  • Tick marks along the path show timing: drag ticks apart to speed that stretch up, or bunch them to slow it down. The timeline's ease curve updates to match.
  • Rotation keys (where a camera looks) show as a small arrow on the key you can aim.
Stays in panels

Everything else

  • The timeline, properties and the File menu stay 2D in the bottom panel and top bar.
  • The Parts pop-up and Assets panel stay 2D.
  • The overlay shows only the selected thing's paths, plus faint paths for anything else on the same timeline.

The frame (the user's design)

The editor looks as the user's redesign draws it (docs/ui/HANDOFF.md, its boards in docs/ui/design/boards, tokens and icons). A 44 px top bar holds the BEVERLY menu (File, Edit, View, Play, Help), the eight tabs (Level, Characters, Vehicles, UI/HUD, Audio, Effects, Screens, Animatics, each with an icon) and Parts, Undo, Redo, Snap and the window's own minimise, maximise and close. The 3D view is one outline set into the frame: a notch dips in at the top for the tab's topics (Level: All, Physics, Lighting, Cameras; Water, Foliage and World come later) and one rises at the bottom for Saved and the shortcut hints. The world renders under the whole window and the frame is translucent over it. Each tab has its own frame colour (where you are) and accent (what you can change). Status pills sit at the view's top left; the dock on its right switches between Inspect, the list and Assets, and folds to a rail.

No rasters in the GUI. Every shape, icon and letter is drawn as a vector by Beverly's own small renderer. Each pixel is covered by exactly how much of it the shape covers, worked out from the shape's edges, so edges are as clean as a browser's at any size. Letters come from the fonts' outlines (Roboto, Roboto Mono, Roboto Condensed for the wordmark). The timeline, when it comes, is the redesign's Timeline board, opened from the bottom notch.

Look and feel

Everything you see follows the redesign; the Motion kit gives the movement: its springs that overshoot a little, entrances that fade and grow in, tooltips springing up, the dock's sliding pill and contents, the dynamic island popping open for each action, and inputs whose letters spring in. The kit is React, and Beverly's panels are Rust, so the movement is ported with the same springs and timings.

Rule for all of Beverly's development: build new UI from the redesign's pieces (and add new pieces to it), moving the way the kit does.

Navigation

Two movement modes, Caps Lock to switch

Editor: middle-click orbit around the reticle, Space hand-pan, wheel zoom with SketchUp-style proximity. Builder: first-person no-clip, WASD, instant start/stop, mouse buttons step speed ⅛× to 400×.
  • Caps Lock is only the mode switch in the editor. Text fields ignore its capitals state, and when you leave the editor window it switches Caps Lock back off so other apps aren't left typing in capitals.
  • Tab opens the Parts pop-up in either mode. In Builder movement it frees the cursor while it's open; picking a part closes it and the part rides on the reticle, ready to drop.
  • No separate up / down keys: W flies where you're looking, so look up and press W to climb.
  • The mouse is captured in Builder, so hold Alt to get a cursor for the panels. Letting go puts you back in flight.
  • Mouse 5 steps faster and Mouse 4 slower through the 12 speeds, with Page Up and Page Down doing the same as a backup ([ ] are the brush size, as in Photoshop). The speed strip appears for 2 seconds when it changes, and a small "Fly 5×" pill stays in the corner.
  • Proximity zoom: each wheel notch moves a fixed fraction of the distance to the surface under the cursor, so it slows down as you get close and never passes through.
  • F frames the selection in both modes.
Feel

Widgets that explain themselves

Minimal, colour-coded, hovering widgets around (or through) the selection. Complex operations in stages that nest around the widget, animated.
  • Axes are always red X, green Y (up), blue Z. The operation shows in the shape: arrows move, rings rotate, cubes on the corners scale (the opposite corner stays put; Shift scales from the middle) and cubes on the faces stretch along one axis. During any drag, type a number and Enter sets it exactly (metres, degrees, or times for corners); Esc cancels.
  • Hovering a handle shows a short label of what dragging will do ("Move along X"). While dragging, it shows the live value. Typing a number during a drag sets it exactly.
  • Staged operations grow outward. For example, Array: drag out the count ring, release, a second ring for spacing slides out around it, and Enter confirms. Esc backs out one stage.
  • Part colours match the palette categories everywhere: in the world, in wires, in the bottom panel.
Placement

Bounding boxes and widgets

Parts drop onto the nearest surface below the mouse; a widget lifts them into the air.
  • Each part has a default size. Volume-type parts (triggers, weather zones, kill boxes) show face handles for stretching. Point-type parts (sound, light, spawner) show a radius handle.
  • Dropping aligns the part to the surface it lands on. Hold Shift while dropping to keep it upright instead.
  • Shapes double as colliders. Every basic shape has a Visible switch and a Collision setting (Solid, Walk-through, Trigger). Turn Visible off for a quick invisible wall: in the editor it shows as a hatched see-through outline, and in the game it isn't drawn.
  • Snapping is on by default (0.25 m, 15°) and shown in the top bar. Hold Ctrl to drag freely.
Textures

Drag onto a selected shape

Select the shape, drag a texture onto it; handles scale it proportionally (Shift) or stretch it. Tile repeat, opacity, metalness, roughness.
  • Drag image files from Explorer straight into the Textures panel (or anywhere in the window). They're copied into the folder you drop them on; the originals stay where they were. Models and sounds import the same way.
  • Dropping onto one face textures that face; holding Shift while dropping textures the whole shape (the face under the pointer lights up violet first). A texture keeps its size in metres when the shape is resized, and wraps around round faces (cylinder sides, the arch's opening, the ball).
  • Texture handles sit flat on the face, drawn in a different colour (violet) from the move widget so the two can't be confused: the middle slides the texture, the corners size one copy of it (Shift keeps its shape). The part panel's Textures section has the same numbers.
  • A texture plus its settings saves as a reusable Material in the Assets folder (assets/materials, one made for each texture automatically), so changing it changes every surface that uses it. Click a material in Assets for the Materials panel: texture, tint, opacity, metal, roughness and glow (for screens and light strips), how many surfaces use it, and Copy to make one to change on its own. Materials drag onto faces like textures.
Instancing

A simple rule for what gets instanced

Non-script, non-character parts are instanced unless toggled Unique. Foliage gets its own system later.
  • Rule: anything that is only a mesh plus a material (shapes and imported models with no wires, no skeleton, no behaviour) is instanced. Script parts, characters, lights and cameras are always individual.
  • The box and its contents: each part is a box (name, position, turn, size), placed like a SketchUp component instance, and instances share everything inside it: the shape or model, its colour, the textures and how they lie, Visible, Collision, surface tags. Parts with the same contents are instances of each other; changing one's contents asks "Change all 12, or make this one Unique?" (Enter / U), with the others outlined. One undo takes back the change and the answer.
  • Unique (a toggle in the part panel) gives a part contents of its own: its changes never ask, and it is never changed with others. Material files stay shared assets (the Materials panel's Copy makes one of its own).
  • Drawing: parts sharing a mesh and a material are batched by Bevy. One mesh per shape, one loaded copy per model, and textured shapes share one mesh per shape, size and textured faces.
  • Scatter later: the brush and the pen scatter trees, bushes, grass, rocks and other props from a set of variants with random rotation, scale and tint, so repeats aren't obvious. They're always instanced, always LOD'd, and impostors far away.
Tabs and topics

Make it in its tab, place it in the Level

Redundant ways to get somewhere are fine, as long as they don't conflict. A right-click menu in the Level tab such as "Edit this in the ___ Effects tab".
  • The rule: each tab makes one kind of thing (Characters: characters; Vehicles: vehicles; UI/HUD: the HUD; Audio: sounds and music; Effects: effects; Screens: menus; Animatics: cut scenes). The Level tab places and arranges them, and shapes the level itself, so its topics are about the level: All · Physics · Lighting · Cameras · Terrain · Foliage · World. Terrain takes over the Water topic (water is part of the ground); World holds what is level-wide (sky, the kind of ground, wind, weather, boundaries).
  • Getting from a placed thing to where it is made (all of these, none conflicting):
    • Right-click it: Edit Runner in Characters, Edit its effects in Effects › Character effects, Edit its sounds in Audio, plus the usual duplicate, delete, rename, make unique. Right-click on empty ground or sky offers the level's World and Terrain topics and World effects.
    • Inspect: a thing's groups end with links to where it is made (an Effects group listing its effects, each with Edit ↗).
    • Double-click = open what it is made of, everywhere: a timeline clip, a screen card, a group or prefab, and a placed character or vehicle (its tab).
    • The tab bar and Ctrl 1–8 (in tab order).
    • After any jump, a back chip (a status pill, top left: ← Level · Runner, Alt ←) returns to where you were, with the same selection and view.
  • Keys stay per tab: a letter may mean different things in different tabs (Characters' P poses, the Level's P is the pen); within a tab each key has one job. Kept everywhere: Tab parts, Caps Lock movement mode, [ ] fly speed, ` test, T timeline, F frame, Esc deselect or leave a tool.
  • Right mouse: a plain right-click opens the menu; while a tool or placing is active it leaves it (as it already cancels placing); Alt + right-drag sizes the brush. No tool erases with the right button: erasing is Alt with the tool.
  • Handles: everything you can drag to change (texture handles, curve handles, key tangents) is drawn in the tab's accent (the Level's violet), and only the active tool's handles show, so they never overlap.
Effects tab

Topics by what the effect is on, each with a stage

Topics like Character Effects, Vehicle Effects, Object Effects and World Effects, each with a viewing stage for context. How you select them, with the least friction, and a right-click "Edit this in ___ Effects".
  • Topics: Character effects · Vehicle effects · Object effects · World effects. A topic is where you look at an effect, not where it is stored: an effect is one file (assets/effects) that can sit on many things (the same dust kick on a runner's feet and a buggy's wheels).
  • Stages: Character: the chosen character on a turntable, its joints shown, playing its moves (run, jump, land) so the effects fire on their events. Vehicle: the chosen vehicle on a short test track with throttle, boost and skid. Object: the chosen part or model on a plinth with its triggers (hit, break, open, pick up). World: a patch of ground under the sky, with time of day, wind and rain sliders. The stage's subject is picked with an On: Runner ▾ chip (or a stand-in when the effect is for anything); a topic whose subjects don't exist yet waits as "Coming soon".
  • Kinds become layers: an effect is layers of Plume, Particles, Marks (decals), Glow, Trail, Shake and a short post-processing touch (a flash, heat haze, a hit vignette), timed together. The dock's list shows effects grouped by what they are on; the Inspect panel shows the selected layer.
  • Looks versus moments: a level's or a camera's lasting look (bloom, colour grading) is set in the Level tab (World and Cameras); a momentary touch fired by an event is an effect layer here. Weather is made here (what the rain looks like) and placed in the Level (zones and the World topic choose it).
  • Getting here: right-click a thing in the Level, its Inspect Effects group, its Characters or Vehicles tab (their Effects group), double-click an effect file in Assets, or the tab bar; the back chip returns.
Audio tab

Make sounds and music in the tab, place them in the Level

Plan the Audio tab, with research. Adding sounds to characters, sets and weather needs attention (2026-11-15).
  • Topics (five): Sounds · Music · Ambience · Mixer · Voices. Cyan frame, coral accent, the wave icon (the redesign's tab table). Voices waits as "Coming soon" until dialog.
  • A sound is a file (assets/sounds/*.bev), like a material: one or more clips (your recordings, or generated sounds later), how one is picked (at random without repeats, or in turn), volume, pitch, how much both vary each time, range (full volume near, silent far), loop, and how many may play at once. Sets per surface live in the same file: a footstep sound holds Grass, Metal and Water sets and the surface underfoot picks one (the surfaces: Stone, Wood, Metal, Grass, Dirt, Sand, Gravel, Water, Snow, Carpet). Recordings are the user's own; nothing is baked; waveforms are drawn as vector lines worked out when shown.
  • Stages: Sounds: a speaker on a plinth with its range drawn as rings; walk or orbit around it to hear the falloff, or pick On: Runner ▾ to hear it on that subject's moves (steps on the chosen surface, a jump, a landing). Music: the shared timeline, stems as tracks and sections as regions, with a mood chip to try changes. Ambience: a patch of world under the sky with time of day, wind and rain sliders (the Effects tab's World stage), scattered sounds popping up as dots around you. Mixer: one strip per bus with its meter.
  • Placing: a Sound part (a speaker, from the parts pop-up or by dropping an audio file into the view) plays on start or when wired (Play, Stop), heard in 3D: from the camera while editing, from the player in play. Characters, vehicles, parts and effects get a Sounds group that names what plays on which event. Zone parts and the World topic choose a place's ambience, reverb and music.
  • Mixer: buses Music, Effects, Voices, Ambience and Interface (the Settings screen's volume sliders), ducking (voices lower the music), reverb spaces (Room, Hall, Cave, Outdoors), sounds muffled behind walls and pitched by speed, and moods that change the mix for a while (Underwater, Paused, Hurt).
  • Music: stems fade in and out with an intensity, sections change on the next bar or beat, stingers play on events (how FMOD and Wwise do adaptive music); a Music action and variables drive it.
  • Getting here: the tab bar or Ctrl 5 (each tab keeps its own view; the level waits hidden), double-click a sound file in Assets, right-click a Sound part or a thing with sounds ("Edit this sound in the Audio tab"), the Sounds group's edit button, or the tab bar; the back chip returns.
  • Engine (researched 2026-11-17): kira 0.12, used directly through a small layer of Beverly's own: it has spatial tracks with a listener, sends for reverb, filters, clocks for music on the beat, and loop regions. bevy_kira_audio (Bevy 0.20) hides the clocks and sends; bevy_seedling (Firewheel, planned as Bevy's own audio) has no Bevy 0.20 release yet. Sounds are stored as settings, so the engine can change later.
Brush, pen and fill

One set of tools in every tab

Precision placement of single items, brushes from small amounts up to whole forests, spline tools with orderly or random spacing, a bucket that fills by the colour clicked; the brush and Bezier tool reused across Foliage, Assets, Characters, Vehicles and more. Something must control what is under the foliage too.
  • Four tools, the same keys everywhere: Place one item exactly (on the surface, upright or tilted to it; wheel turns, Shift+wheel scales; snapping as today). Brush from a 10 cm dab to a 500 m forest (S / M / L / XL presets, the slider, Alt + right-drag in the view), with strength as Add / Subtract (each pass a little) or Set (exactly this density, however often you go over it). Pen (Bezier). Fill (the bucket). Plus Pick (take what is under the cursor) and erasing as each tool's other side (hold Alt). Keys: V Place, B Brush, P Pen, G Fill, I Pick; [ ] brush smaller / larger and Shift+[ ] softer / harder, as in Photoshop (user, 2026-11-17); 1–4 the presets S to XL while the brush is out.
  • The pen's modes: Even (one every N metres, facing along it: fences, lamp posts, avenues, hedges), Scattered (random in a band of a given width that thins at its edges: forest edges, stream banks), Both sides, and a closed curve fills inside (a lasso). A curve stays live (move a point and the hedge follows); Turn into items makes them placed items to nudge one by one.
  • Fill matches a rule: the same material or texture (a blue square on concrete: only the blue gets grass), the same colour (with a tolerance), the same surface tag, everything inside this part, or this whole floor; Connected only stops at gaps. The fill stays a rule: recolour the square and the grass follows.
  • Rules any tool carries: slope limit, height band, keep clear of paths, roads and water, spacing between items, which surfaces it may land on.
  • The work is saved, not the result: strokes, curves and fills are small objects (points, settings, a seed) and everything is rebuilt from them, so no images are stored, a forest is one stroke, old strokes stay selectable and editable, and undo and the MCP cover all of it.
  • Each tab says what the tools place: Foliage: grass, plants and Mixes. Level and Assets: props, rubble, fences, cables. Vehicles: roads, rails, routes, traffic. Later: terrain heights and surfaces, rivers, navigation, weather zones, lights along a curve, sound areas.
  • Characters tab (to plan with it): the brush paints Beverly's own shaders onto a character; the pen draws hair, strips of cloth and jewellery that move with physics.
Foliage

The Level tab's Foliage topic

A tab for foliage (the redesign has it as the Level tab's Foliage topic); grass on the tops of things, even walls or ceilings if painted; built right after Instancing.
  • The view: the level, parts dimmed and not selectable, so every click paints. The brush shows as rings on the surface (its size, and where the soft edge starts).
  • The dock's list: Mixes first, then grass and ground-cover species, then plant sets (scattered models, which come with LOD and impostors). "+ New" copies one to change.
  • Mixes: one pick for a whole look: a ground material, grass species with weights, plant sets with their spacing. Meadow: soil, grass 70%, daisies 10%, tall grass 20%. Forest floor: leaf litter, ivy, ferns, trees 6 to 10 m apart. Riverbank: wet mud, reeds and rushes. The ground layer belongs to the Mix, with a per-stroke override (the same meadow, muddier here).
  • What is under the foliage: a stroke lays a ground layer (a material: soil, moss, leaf litter) and the plants together. The ground's Spread says how far it reaches past the plants, its edge is soft, and it follows density (thin grass shows the soil through). Grass takes the colour of the surface at its roots. Ground-only strokes are plain surface painting. The stroke can tag what it covers (grass, mud) for footsteps, hiding in grass and slipping.
  • The grass carpet (the user's grass system, ported from their game, checked 2026-11-17: it draws, follows its centre without swimming, and the species match): one instanced lattice that exists only in a circle around the camera or player, every blade placed, shaped, coloured and swayed in the shader; 17 species as data. It reads where the ground is and what to grow from textures built while playing, from the strokes and the surfaces under them (part tops, and walls or ceilings where painted), never stored. One height per spot: grass under a bridge and on it needs a second layer; walls and ceilings may need plants laid on the surface instead of the carpet's upright blades.
  • Ground relief: flat colour and texture for now. Researched 2026-11-17 for later: wgpu has no tessellation stage, but we can build our own on the GPU when it is needed (user), the way the terrain is drawn: rings of grid around the camera, finer close up, shaped in the shader; Bevy's materials already do parallax occlusion (depth from the user's own height texture, cost per pixel, best close up, odd on bent surfaces, no silhouettes or shadows); terrain can be displaced for real in its own mesh; a bump from noise in the shader is nearly free; leaf litter is cheapest as flat cards lying on the ground (the carpet's lily-pad and ivy kinds already do this); wet mud is a darker, glossier ground with no geometry at all.
Terrain

A Terrain topic for the ground itself

  • Everything about the ground's shape and skin in one place (the level-wide choice of ground, none, flat floor, terrain or ocean, stays in the World topic with the sky): Surfaces (the ground materials Mixes use, and ground-only painting with the same tools, on parts and the flat floor before any terrain exists), then sculpting (raise, lower, smooth, flatten; ridges and beds with the pen; the ground is drawn from its description on the GPU, not stored as meshes), water (the ocean's waves, lakes, rivers: it takes over the Water topic), roads and caves as they come. Foliage stays its own topic and borrows the Surfaces.
Surface tags

Say what a surface is

  • The TAGS column of the part panel: chips for Climbable, Diggable, Water, Grind rail, Perch spot and Slippery, any number at once. Tags are contents, so instances share them; the player start and lights have none.
  • Slippery works now: on it the walker gathers speed slowly and slides on after you let go. The others wait for what reads them: climbing, digging, grinding and perching (traversal), swimming (water), birds landing (the flock).
  • Saved in the level as a list (tags: [Climbable, Slippery]), and set by name through the MCP server.
LOD

Automatic by default

  • On import, the editor makes 3 simpler versions of each model automatically. Swaps happen by how big the object is on screen, not by metres, so big things stay detailed further out.
  • When you select a model, faint coloured rings on the ground show where each swap happens. Drag a ring to move that swap point.
  • View menu → "Colour by LOD" tints the whole level so heavy spots stand out.
  • Swaps cross-fade instead of popping (Bevy's visibility ranges support this).
  • The last level is an octahedral impostor: one camera-facing card baked from views all around the model, keeping its 3D look from every angle (ported from the user's baker, docs/reference/octahedral-impostor-baker.html). Made on import, with a toggle; its quality can change any time and it rebakes in the background. The atlases are cached, never stored in the project.
  • An LOD drawer pops out beside Assets: the project's LOD settings, and for a selected model each level side by side as small live views you can turn, with triangle counts, swap sizes and a mesh / impostor compare.
  • Later, virtual geometry, Tessera (Nanite-like, docs/tessera.md): dense models drawn cluster by cluster with seamless detail and GPU culling, from a fork of Bevy's meshlet renderer. Classic LOD stays for skinned and see-through models and as the last fallback; a 32-bit path keeps the seamless detail on cards without 64-bit atomics.
Shaders

Any shader, written by hand or by Claude

  • Shaders are project assets (assets/shaders/*.wesl): a settings header turns their values into sliders, edits reload at once and errors show in the island with the line.
  • Any material can use one. Moving vertices (wind, wobble) and cut-out edges (leaves) are declared as raster hooks, so the normal renderer and virtual geometry both draw them; see-through shaders are marked so they take the normal path.
  • The MCP server can write, list and assign shaders.
Prepared models

A model's own file, made ready in its tab

  • Each imported model gets its own file beside it: collision, scale, materials, LOD and tags, plus lights, sounds, decals and effects that come with every copy. Changing it changes every copy, as materials do.
  • Edit it in the tab that knows it (a vehicle in Vehicles, a tree in Foliage, a character in Characters), then place it in the Level. Assets shows where it is prepared and chips for its LODs, impostor and virtual geometry.
  • Faster placing: your models in the parts pop-up, drag from Assets into the view, Shift keeps placing.
Draw distance

Tied to the haze

  • One level-wide far distance, plus size classes: small props vanish first, then medium, then large. Landmarks are always visible.
  • The haze end is linked to the far distance, so objects fade into haze instead of popping out at the edge.
  • Any part can be flagged "Always visible".
  • In Builder movement, a faint ground circle shows the current far distance around you.
Wiring

Noodles in the world, sequences for chains

  • Outputs are events ("On enter", "Done") and inputs are actions ("Start", "Reset"). Drag from an output to any part and pick the input from a small pop-up.
  • Wires are drawn as arcs in the 3D world. W opens a flat node-graph view for when parts are far apart.
  • Your example chain is one straight line, so a Sequence part holds it as a list: steps and waits, reordered by dragging. That avoids eight separate noodles. Noodles are for branching and logic.
  • Variables (flags and counters such as "has key" or "enemies left") are parts too. Save points store them.
  • Moves is a setting on any shape: an end position and a time. Its inputs are Go, Back and Toggle, its outputs Arrived and Returned, and the player rides it. A lift is a Plane that moves plus an Interact button wired to its Toggle.
  • In test mode, wires pulse when they fire, so you can see why something did or didn't happen.
  • Select several parts → "Make prefab" packs them into one part with only the chosen ports showing. For example, an ambush prefab you can reuse.
Lighting

Real-time, with honest widgets

  • Each level has its Lighting, shown in the bottom panel when nothing is selected: the sun (its direction round the level, its height above the horizon, colour, brightness and shadows) and a fill light (flat light from every side, with its colour, so shade is never black). "Interior" turns the sun off, leaving the fill plus your own lights. The sky and its time-of-day slider come with Sky and clouds.
  • Light parts (the Lights section of the parts pop-up): point (a sphere showing its reach, with a handle to size it) and spot (its cone drawn: drag the tip to aim it and set its reach, the rim to widen or narrow it). A spot shines away from the surface it is placed on, so on a ceiling it points down. Each has a colour, brightness, reach and shadows on or off; the editor shows a small bulb to click, the game only the light. Lights are always individual and never collide. Plus a glowing-surface material for screens and strips.
  • Reflection box parts for shiny areas. Their influence box is always drawn, so a stray reflection, like the mech's silver square, is easy to find.
  • A light budget meter in the view's top-right corner shows the most shadowed lights reaching one place, against a budget of 4; it turns red when over, says which lights they are, and clicking it selects them in turn. It shows only while the level has shadowed lights.
  • Cheaper lights later (docs/lighting.md): shadows cached until something in a light's reach moves (aim: 20 shadowed lights without notice), still lights baked into light probes and reflection boxes (kept in the bake cache, never the project), and ray-traced realtime GI on cards that can.
Cameras

Camera parts for cinematics, plus the player's camera

  • The player's camera belongs to the character, for example Last Ascent's third-person rig. It's set up once per character, not per level. Play always looks through it.
  • Camera parts are for cinematics (the views Animatics cuts between while recording), never a view the game switches to while playing (user, 2026-11-15). In play they are neither drawn nor solid.
  • Kinds, in the Cameras section of the parts pop-up: Fixed (stays put), Orbit (circles a target you drag by its handle, once a Lap, always looking at it) and Follow (keeps the offset from the player start it was placed at, so it travels with the character, third-person). Rail cameras (one that travels along a path you draw) come with Paths. Each has a View (how much it sees, top to bottom) and shows its picture as a wire pyramid (16:9, a notch on top); the selected Orbit camera also shows its circle, a Follow camera a line to the player start. A placed camera stands at eye height, looking the way you look.
  • "Look through" (L, or the button in the part panel) puts your view in the camera: fly it like Builder movement to aim it by eye, inside a 16:9 frame with only what the camera sees; Esc goes back, and the whole flight is one undo step.
  • The editor's lines, handles and stand-ins (grid, pyramids, bulbs, the player start, hidden colliders) are on a layer of their own, so a picture through a camera, or from a point for the MCP server, shows only the level.
Weather and haze

Level atmosphere plus zones

  • Level settings: sky, sun, haze colour and density, wind, and precipitation (rain, snow, ash, embers).
  • Zone parts are stretchable boxes that override those settings inside them, blending over a few metres at the edge. "Interior" is a preset zone: no sky, no weather, default lighting.
  • The "Set weather" action changes the level's atmosphere over time, so a storm can roll in on cue.
Game UI (HUD)

A 2D layer over the game view

  • The UI/HUD tab (Ctrl 4, and F4 as you first asked) is where the HUD is edited: its own widgets become selectable over the game view.
  • Widgets: text, image, bar, crosshair, objective, dialog box, minimap. Each pins to one of 9 anchor points and stays inside the safe area.
  • Preview at 16:9, 21:9 and 4:3 from a toggle.
  • Bind by wire: drag from a bar to a value (fuel, health, a counter variable).
  • In-world panels, like the mech's fuel gauge, are the same widgets placed on a surface.
Menus

Screens as a flow

  • The Screens mode shows every screen as a card in a flow chart. Double-click a card to edit it with the same widgets as the HUD, plus buttons, sliders, toggles and lists.
  • Buttons wire to actions: Go to screen, Start level, Continue, Quit, Change setting.
  • New projects start with a working Settings screen (graphics, audio, key rebinding), since every game needs one.
  • A screen's background can be a live 3D scene, a level seen through one of its camera parts.
Animatics

A mode built on the shared timeline

Another mode. Macro recording of the main character. Views: 1st person, 3rd person, other cameras, an NPC's view.
  • One track per actor: cameras, characters, lights, sounds, subtitles and script events. Cutting between camera tracks gives you 1st person, 3rd person, other cameras or an NPC's eyes.
  • Recording: you play the character and the editor records the resulting movement and animation, not the key presses. Replaying recorded keys through physics drifts, so a recording could come out differently every time.
  • Recorded tracks become editable keyframes, so you can trim or retime them.
  • Played by the "Animatic" action. Letterboxing and "skippable" are per-animatic switches.
Testing

Play from the reticle

` tests the game with the main character at the reticle point. F4 swaps editor UI for game UI.
  • The editor snapshots the level when you press `, and pressing it again returns you to the exact spot and view you left. Things destroyed or spawned during play come back as they were.
  • Values you tune during play (a light's colour, a timer's length) are offered with a "Keep these changes?" list on return.
  • Shift+` starts from the level's Player start instead of the reticle, to test the real opening.
Demo level

Island to orbit: a cube slice of a cartoon world

A very basic level in a simple cartoony style: a cube slice of a world, a tropical island touching three sides and ocean on the fourth. Jump into the water, or walk, run and jump on land. A simple rocket ship you enter; it lifts off by itself into space above (cartoon proportions) and stops above the atmosphere, the door opens and you come out in a simple space suit, free to move around with 6-DOF, or go back in: it flies back down, lands, the door opens and you pop out on the ground. 50 m wide, 50 m long, 100 m tall, the sea at 25 m.
  • The slice: 50 × 50 m, 100 m tall, held in by the level's boundaries. The island (terrain with a beach, palms from the user's own models or built from shapes) meets the north, east and west edges; the south edge is open ocean, its surface at 25 m and its floor at 0. The ocean's sides at the edges show as a cut-away, as in the sketch.
  • The look: a simple cartoon style: the project's art style set to flat, bright colours with soft cel shading and outlines; the sky fades from a cartoon blue into black with stars above the atmosphere line at 50 m.
  • On the island: walk, run and jump; jump in the sea and swim at the surface or dive.
  • Camera modes: the demo sets its characters so a key switches between first and third person while you are in control, as the islander or the astronaut, to try both modes. How cameras behave is always the creator's choice, set per game and per character, never built in.
  • The rocket: stands on the beach. Press E at its door: the player goes in, the door shuts, it lifts off with a shake, flame and smoke (cartoon proportions: a few metres tall, quick), climbs to about 80 m and stops above the atmosphere. The door opens and the player comes out as the astronaut (a second character file: the same player in a simple space suit).
  • The flights are cutscenes: lift-off and landing are animatics with a third-person camera, set to blend in from the player's camera without a cut, follow the rocket, and blend back out to whichever mode the player was in as the door opens. Blend or cut, how long, and which mode comes back are each animatic's own settings.
  • In space: above 50 m is a zone with no gravity: the suit moves in six directions (forward, back, sideways, up, down and turning freely, with a gentle flight assist so it doesn't drift away). Press E at the rocket's door to go back in: it flies down, lands where it stood, the door opens and the islander pops out on the ground, back in control.
  • Built from: the terrain, ocean, swimming, sky, art styles, zones, boundaries, particles, character files, space movement and the vehicles' enter and exit (the rocket is a vehicle that flies itself along a path), the character camera (first and third person) and animatics; a test project plays the whole round trip in both camera modes.

Things not on the list yet

Basics
  • File menu: New / Open project, New level, Save, Save as, Load level, Import files, Export, Quit. Quitting with unsaved changes asks first.
  • Features: the full feature list in build order, with sizes and parts, is editor/features.json, shown under Help > Features and as docs/roadmap.html. Characters are built from Beverly's own building blocks (no custom Rust parts).
  • Multiplayer later: Beverly is built so multiplayer can be added: game state lives in data, input goes through named actions, effects and sounds react to events.
  • Help menu: About Beverly (which build this is) and Check for updates, which downloads the newest release, checks it and puts it in place; it runs from the next start, with no "Run anyway" from Windows.
  • MCP Server (Help menu, off by default): lets an AI assistant on this computer, such as Claude Code, read the project and parts with every setting; place, change and delete parts with any setting (set from the same descriptions the panels are drawn from) and wire them; take screenshots of the window or from any point and move the view; play-test (start, press keys, read the player and moving parts, stop); run Beverly's named actions (the same list the menus and keys use); and run the scripted tests or an export as background jobs. Every change is one undo step. A token and this computer only; never in exported games.
  • Undo / redo for everything, including wiring and texture moves.
  • Autosave every few minutes, plus a project launcher with templates: Empty, First-person, Third-person.
  • Built-in characters: a generic humanoid and a small cartoon character, rigged and animated, so any project can be tested from day one. Last Ascent swaps in its mech.
  • Scene list (shown under Assets in the wireframe) for finding and hiding things.
Gameplay
  • Collision: per part, Solid, Walk-through or Trigger. Hidden shapes are plain colliders.
  • Physics (Avian, the one collision world while playing, on a fixed 60 Hz step drawn smoothed): each Solid part is Still (things stand on it and bump into it) or Loose (falls and tumbles: crates, barrels, with a Weight and a Grip), a switch in the part panel shared by instances; characters push loose parts (light ones fly, heavy ones don't budge) and stand on them, and loose parts at rest sleep until touched; blasts throw them outward and a winch drags one toward a point; a part that Moves is a moving body that carries what stands on it. Still parts collide exactly (boxes as boxes, everything else triangle for triangle); loose ones as a hull, or convex pieces for concave shapes such as stairs, arches and models. When play stops, everything goes back where it was placed.
  • The walker (the stand-in character) is a floating character (bevy_tnua) in the same physics world: it walks up steps and slopes, rides moving parts and slides on Slippery ones. A game's own character collides through the same world, as a body of its own or by asking Avian.
  • Enemy paths: a navigation area enemies can walk, built from the level with one button, across floors, stairs and ramps, with paths found on it; patrol paths are drawn with the Paths tool. The level panel's Navigation column says who it is for (width, height, the step and slope they climb), builds it and says how it stands (square metres, separate areas, out of date once the level changes); the area is drawn over the level in green. Try a path there: click a start, then an end, and the walk between them is drawn (yellow, with its length), or a red line says there is no way. Play works the area out when it starts, so characters can ask it for paths. It is never stored, only rebuilt from the level.
  • Paths tool for rails, patrols, camera paths and roads, drawn with the pen.
  • Brush and pen: a round brush (size, hardness soft to hard as in Photoshop, strength, spacing) and a Bezier pen that draw on terrain, water and parts. They lay textures, raise and carve terrain, or scatter props, and are stored as strokes and curves, never paint images.
  • Asset library: rename, duplicate, delete (to a trash folder undo can bring back) and move assets; everything using one follows it; used-by counts and Find in level.
  • Input map: actions such as Jump or Fire mapped to keys and gamepad, editable in Settings.
  • Save system: what a save point stores (player, variables, which parts already fired).
Sound and shipping
  • Audio zones: ambient loops, music regions and reverb boxes, blended like weather zones.
  • Export panel: "Make Windows zip" locally, or "Build on GitHub". Both produce the same zip, carrying the baked data the level used (kept locally in a bake cache while you work, never in the project).
  • Performance meter: View > Performance meter: frames a second, frame time, the card's time, triangles, meshes in view, lights, entities and memory, coloured green, amber or red. Scripted tests print the same numbers.
  • Several levels per project, plus level exits wired to the next level.

Minimum to start

Each step is usable by itself. The first one is enough to start blocking out a level.
